🛒 Kiran Bazaar: Project Explainer

CT-378 Machine Learning · plain-language guide to what the project does, for the group and the viva
In one sentence: given a city (Karachi, Lahore, Multan, Peshawar, Quetta) and a food staple (atta, rice, sugar, oil, ghee, milk, eggs, chicken, daals), the system predicts the probability that its price will rise by more than 5 % next month, e.g. "Wheat flour (atta) in Lahore: 16 % chance of a >5 % jump in October 2026".

1. What problem are we solving? Classification or regression?

Main task = binary CLASSIFICATION. For every city + staple + month, the answer is Yes (spike) or No (no spike):

  • Spike = 1 if next month's price is more than 5 % higher than this month's price; otherwise 0.
  • The model outputs a probability (0-100 %). If it is above 27% (our tuned alert threshold), the app says "spike likely".

Extra task = REGRESSION (a smaller side model): predict the expected % change in price, which the app turns into "expected price next month: Rs 111.9".

Why classification as the main task? Month-to-month price changes are close to random, so predicting the exact number is very hard (our regression only barely beats "no change"). Predicting the event users care about ("will it jump?") is learnable and gives clean metrics (F1, ROC-AUC). It also differs from the published work on this dataset, which used regression.

3. How the raw data becomes a training table

  • Each row = one city × one staple × one month, e.g. "Lahore, Sugar, March 2023".
  • Time window used: Jan 2014 to Aug 2026 (before 2014 only rice and wheat were recorded, and fuel/wage data did not exist). Result: 9,568 labelled rows; ~15 % of them are spikes.
  • 26 input features, all computed using only the current and past months (never the future), plus 5 more created inside the pipeline:
GroupFeatures (plain words)
Price momentum% change over the last 1, 2, 3, 6 and 12 months; price vs its 3- and 12-month average; price vs its 12-month high

Cost driversdiesel change (1 and 3 months), petrol change, labour wage change, USD/PKR change (1 and 3 months)

4. How we trained and tested (the ML lifecycle)

  1. EDA (notebooks/01_eda.ipynb): 7 plots covering data coverage/missing values, distributions, price vs rupee trend, outliers, class balance, correlations, seasonality.
  2. Pre-processing inside a scikit-learn Pipeline: fill missing values (median, plus missing-value flags) → scale numbers (StandardScaler) → one-hot encode text columns. Fitted on training data only, so there is no data leakage.
  3. Time-ordered split (70/15/15): train = 2014-01 to 2022-11 (6,646 rows) · validation = 2022-12 to 2024-09 (1,427) · test = 2024-10 to 2026-08 (1,495). We never train on the future and test on the past.
  4. Tuning: GridSearchCV / RandomizedSearchCV with 5-fold time-series cross-validation, optimising ROC-AUC.
  5. Model selection on validation → best model picked; alert threshold tuned for best F1 → retrained on train+validation → tested once on the test set.
  6. Saved with joblib in models/; versions pinned in requirements.txt.

Unsupervised algorithms (notebook 03, analysis only)

  • PCA: 10 components keep 90 % of the information; the first component is a "price momentum" direction.
  • K-Means (k = 4): groups the 65 city-item series into volatile perishables (chicken, eggs), rupee-sensitive items (daals, rice, wheat), rare-jump items (ghee, oil) and stable items (milk, sugar).
  • Apriori: finds items that spike together. Ghee ↔ cooking oil (lift ≈ 7, both use imported palm oil), the three daals, and wheat ↔ atta.

Reading the confusion matrix

  • 110 spikes correctly predicted (true positives)
  • 78 spikes missed (false negatives)
  • 154 false alarms (false positives)
  • 1153 calm months correctly predicted (true negatives)
Why we don't lead with accuracy: only ~13 % of test months are spikes, so a useless model that always says "no spike" would score ~87 % accuracy. That's why the guideline asks for F1 / ROC-AUC / confusion matrix, and why we report those.

Is it good enough? Yes for the project. The guidelines mark the quality of the lifecycle, not a minimum score. The model clearly beats both baselines (ROC-AUC 0.82 vs 0.62; F1 0.49 vs 0.33). Food prices one month ahead are partly random, so 0.82 is a solid result. We set the alert threshold to 27% (not 50 %) on purpose: for an early-warning tool it is better to catch most spikes and accept some false alarms.

Regression on test: MAE 3.49 percentage points vs 3.60 for "no change", R² 0.05. That is only a small improvement, which confirms that classification was the right main framing.

7. Over-fitting / under-fitting

Train vs validation ROC-AUC
  • Over-fitting: KNN (train 1.00 → val 0.69) memorises the training data; Gradient Boosting (0.96 → 0.73) also over-fits.
  • Under-fitting: Logistic Regression and Naive Bayes have small gaps but low scores; they are too simple for the non-linear pattern.
  • Bagging (0.89 → 0.76) is the best balance.
  • Validation (Dec 2022 to Sep 2024) is harder than training because of the 2022-24 inflation shock: spike rate 19 % vs 15 %.

9. Limitations (be ready to say these yourselves)

  1. Only 5 big cities and monthly data. Small towns and weekly movements are not covered.
  2. Uneven skill across items: good for chicken, moong daal, cooking oil and milk; close to random for rice, mash daal and eggs. Part of the overall 0.82 comes from simply telling volatile items from stable ones.
  3. No shock information: floods, government wheat support-price announcements, import bans, bird flu and Ramadan/Eid timing are not in the features.
  4. Regime shifts: the spike rate changes over time (15 % → 19 % → 13 %), so probabilities can drift; the model should be retrained regularly.
  5. Fuel / exchange-rate features were untested in a devaluation period inside the test window.
  6. Regression is weak (R² ≈ 0.05): the "expected price" is a rough guide only.
  7. Data is secondary (collected by PBS/WFP), so we inherit any recording errors and gaps (40-50 % of months are missing for items that start in 2013-15).

Future work: add weekly PBS SPI data and more cities, international wheat and palm-oil prices, a calendar of Eid/Ramadan and policy events, per-item models, and probability calibration.

12. Likely viva questions and short answers

Q: Is this classification or regression?
Classification (spike yes/no, >5 % rise next month). A small regression model gives the expected price as an extra.

Q: Why 5 %?
It marks a real jump that hurts households, yet happens often enough to learn from (~15 % of months). At 10 % only ~4 % of months would be positive.

Q: Why not a random train/test split?
Neighbouring months are almost identical, so a random split leaks future information into training. We split by time and use TimeSeriesSplit for cross-validation.

Q: How did you avoid data leakage?
(1) Features use only current and past months. (2) All pre-processing sits inside the Pipeline, fitted on training data only. (3) Time-ordered split. (4) The test set was used exactly once.

Q: Why is accuracy 84 % but you say F1 0.49?
The classes are imbalanced (≈13 % spikes in test), so accuracy is misleading. F1 and ROC-AUC measure performance on the rare spike class.

Q: Why Bagging and not Random Forest?
Both are tree ensembles. Bagging had higher validation ROC-AUC (0.755 vs 0.739) and PR-AUC. We selected on validation, not on test.

Q: Why is the threshold 27 % and not 50 %?
With imbalanced classes the 0.5 default misses most spikes. We chose the threshold that maximised F1 on the validation set.

Q: How did you handle class imbalance?
class_weight="balanced", tuning on ROC-AUC instead of accuracy, and the F1-optimal threshold.

Q: What was your biggest mistake and how did you fix it?
The first version used price and exchange-rate levels. These rise for 20 years, so models had to extrapolate (Logistic Regression val ROC-AUC 0.51). Switching to % changes raised it to 0.71.

Q: What are the two data sources?
WFP food prices (with fuel, wage and implied USD/PKR series) and the WFP markets table, both from HDX under CC BY-IGO.

Q: What do PCA, K-Means and Apriori add?
PCA shows the signal is spread over ~10 directions; K-Means groups items by price behaviour; Apriori shows which items spike together (ghee and oil, wheat and atta).
